You are eight, translating hydro bills at the kitchen table while your mother stirs something that smells like home and somewhere else at once. Your father holds your report card like scripture, his face deciding whether to be proud. In Toronto winters you wore the same plaid uniform as everyone at the small school, where one teacher knew your grandfather's name before you said it, and shame sat next to belonging in the same pew. Your cousin's report card came first, always better, a ruler you measured your spine against. But your grandmother's kitchen forgave everything, her hands rolling dough, asking nothing of you but your presence. There, you were not the bridge, not the proof, not the debt. Just a child, flour on your fingers, the radiator ticking like a small patient heart.
