## Fragment
Your mother kept a pot of dal on low flame most afternoons, the smell settling into the curtains, into you. You could cry in front of her and the crying had somewhere to go. Your father came home at seven, took off his shoes in the same order, asked about marks before he asked about anything else. Love, in that house, was a ledger kept in his handwriting.

At school in Mumbai, the boys wore watches that cost more than a teacher's month. You learned early that real money doesn't raise its voice.

Your cousin got the marks, the medals, the praise that arrived like weather. You measured yourself against her shadow and always came up short.

Then your grandmother's hip gave out, and suddenly you were the one who remembered her pills, her tea, her silences.

You liked being needed more than you liked being loved. It asked less of you.
