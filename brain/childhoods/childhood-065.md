You learned your father's moods from the sound of his key in the door, the way it turned slow or fast, and you adjusted your body before he crossed the threshold. Your mother narrated every danger until her fear lived in your chest like a second heartbeat. At the small religious school in Miami, you wore a collared uniform and memorized prayers, and one teacher knew enough about your family to make you feel both ashamed and held. Your grandmother's kitchen smelled of garlic and something always simmering, and there you were only a child, nothing more. By ten you were making your sibling's lunch, checking homework, learning the weight of being needed. Then came the day you said no, and the house went quiet in a new way. You still remember that quiet.
