## Fragment
You are nine, measuring rice in a plastic cup because your mother taught you the math once and then forgot she had. She laughs too loud on the phone in her room, door open, and you learn things a daughter should not know. Your father pays for everything and speaks in schedules, in locked drawers, in the click of the porch light at ten. In Bogotá, the bus to school smells like diesel and wet wool, and at the gifted table you trade lunch for silence, because rank is safer than friendship. Then the checks stop coming, and you start reading price tags like sentences. Nobody says why. You just learn to hold your sibling's hand crossing streets, to hold the quiet at dinner, to hold what has no name.
