## Fragment
You learned to read your mother's voice before you learned to read. Nine years old, sitting on the edge of her bed while she cried about money, about your father, about things a child should not have to hold. You became the one who remembered appointments, who signed permission slips in handwriting too neat for your age. Your father wanted trophies, not a son. When you won, he called relatives in Sao Paulo to tell them. When you lost, he left the room.

At the small religious school, the uniform starched, the prayers memorized, one teacher looked at you during silent reading and said you write like someone older than yourself. You have been trying to earn that sentence ever since.

Your cousin was the standard, the one they measured you against at every dinner.

Then your best friend chose someone else.

You still keep score quietly, in rooms where no one is grading you.
