You grew up in Mexico City, in an apartment that smelled of chlorine cleaner and warm tortillas. Your mother counted your steps to school, narrated every car, every stranger, until her fear sat in your chest like a second heartbeat. Your father paid for everything and said little, love folded into bills stacked on the table. At the big public school, you rode the bus at dawn, tested into the gifted track, learned rank at a lunch table before you learned algebra. By eleven you were making your sibling's lunch, checking homework, turning off the stove your mother forgot. Then the year split into before and after, a door closing somewhere down the hall, your father's chair empty at dinner. You said no to the college they'd already chosen. It cost you a silence that lasted months. You still remember the quiet that followed, how it finally felt like yours.
