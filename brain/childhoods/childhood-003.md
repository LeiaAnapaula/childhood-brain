You grew up in Guadalajara, in a house where love had to be earned twice. Your father counted your grades like proof of something he needed. When you won, he lifted you onto his shoulders. When you didn't, he went quiet for days. Your mother's moods moved through the rooms like weather, and you learned to read them before she spoke, footsteps on tile, a door closed too softly. At the small religious school, you wore a stiff white collar and memorized prayers you didn't understand, and one teacher who knew your grandmother by name made you feel both ashamed and held. Then the money went, quietly, a smaller apartment, a mother counting coins at the register. You learned to notice prices before you understood why. You learned to be smart, because smart was safe. You learned to be quiet in rooms where the walls seemed to be listening too.
