## Fragment
You learned to read your mother's moods before you learned to read. Nine years old, sitting on the edge of her bed while she cried about money, about men, about a life she never asked for, and you patted her hand the way she should have patted yours. Your father was a photograph on the shelf, a voice on birthdays. You built a version of him who listened, who existed only when you needed him to. In Lima, the rains came in gray sheets, and the summer you were eleven, no one was home for weeks. You made the empty apartment into a country with its own laws. At school, the nuns taught shame and called it grace. One teacher, grading your essay, wrote in the margin: you notice things most people miss. You have been trying to deserve that sentence ever since.
